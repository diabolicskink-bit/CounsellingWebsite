# Craft review

Use this reference only after a complete draft exists, or when feedback says
copy is generic, awkward, over-written, lifeless, or AI-sounding. Judge the
visible passage in its page context. A sound rationale cannot rescue bad prose.

## Test the thought

1. State what the passage actually says, without describing its topics or
   purpose. If the answer is only "Joel offers counselling for these things",
   the draft probably has coverage but no developed thought.
2. Identify the sentence with the most value. If the writer's explanation is
   more interesting than that sentence, the useful idea has not reached the
   copy.
3. Check whether research distinctions, reader segments, search terms, or
   source headings have become the composition. Return those structures to
   working notes.
4. Ask whether length came from a second useful proposition or from examples,
   symptoms, qualifications, reassurance, and completion added around a thin
   one.
5. Ask what honest reason the passage gives a suitable visitor to choose Joel,
   remember the practice, or continue. A neutral service inventory is not a
   persuasive case.
6. Identify the creative decision. If the passage merely paraphrases the brief
   in polished sentences, return to the opening or organising move and make a
   stronger choice.

## Test the relationship to the reader

- Do not guess what the reader feels, fears, hopes, or has experienced.
- Do not replace that guesswork with a report about "people", "clients", or
  Joel's caseload. Name a supported situation, concern, choice, fact, or
  boundary instead.
- Do not use inclusive breadth as the point of a sentence. A sentence designed
  to account for every possible relationship to a subject usually reads like
  policy or audience analysis.
- Do not reproduce the balance, caveats, synthesis language, or detached voice
  of audience research. Research can suggest what deserves attention; it does
  not determine how public copy should argue or sound.
- Do not make identities, relationship structures, diagnoses, or communities
  parallel members of a presenting-problem list.

## Test the lines

- Keep a clear grammatical subject and main verb. Choose them according to
  whose or what story the sentence should tell.
- Replace broad nouns such as `context`, `experience`, `support`, `space`,
  `pattern`, and `process` when the copy can name the concrete referent;
  otherwise remove the claim.
- Check implication as well as literal accuracy. Do not deepen a fact into
  causation, intimacy, expertise, safety, universality, or an outcome.
- Read aloud for wording the speaker would not naturally assemble, balanced
  clauses that sound composed rather than spoken, repeated three-part shapes,
  and sentences that continue after their useful payload.
- Look for an earned moment of surprise, tension, wit, image, compression, or
  structural interest. Do not add decoration to pass this test; strengthen the
  idea and its expression.
- Delete generic transitions, benefit conversions, explanations of obvious
  implications, summaries, and emotional landings.

## Repair in causal order

Repair the creative commitment and governing idea first, then emphasis,
grammatical centre, structure, specificity, and line construction. Ask for a
missing fact only when it genuinely prevents an honest recommendation.
Preserve a blunt useful sentence over a smooth empty one. If a classification,
list, or provider summary caused the passage, do not merely disguise it with
different wording.

After revision, read only the clean copy once more. Stop when further changes
would be preference rather than a material improvement.
