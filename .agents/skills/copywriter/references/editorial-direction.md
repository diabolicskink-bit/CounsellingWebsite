# Editorial direction

Use this reference before substantial strategy, drafting, or rewriting, and
whenever the reader scope, page purpose, artifact register, or direction is
uncertain. Use it to make the choices that govern the draft, not to create a
visible planning ritual.

## Establish the source envelope

Classify the available material by the authority it has in this task:

| Input | Treatment |
| --- | --- |
| Exact owner-approved wording | Preserve it. Flag a consequential concern rather than silently polishing it. |
| Supplied practice information or repository-owned fact | Re-express it for the artifact without changing its meaning. |
| Speaker-owned observation, judgement, question, or phrase | Use it as positive material for point of view and voice. |
| Relevant audience research | Use it to test assumptions and decisions, not as public testimony or a content quota. |
| Natural search or specialist language | Use it where it improves identification, retrieval, or precision. |
| Rough direction or brainstorm | Develop or reject it according to the selected commitment; do not preserve accidental syntax. |
| Existing public copy | Use it for facts, layout, and surrounding context unless explicitly approved as wording. |
| Unknown, unsupported idea, or conflict | Omit, mark, or return for an owner decision; never resolve it through plausible prose. |

Identify what has already been communicated, what belongs elsewhere, and what
the artifact may leave unsaid. Distinctiveness must come from supported
selection, judgement, relationship, or treatment. If the envelope cannot
support a particular treatment, write useful plain provisional copy and expose
the gap.

Do not search for anecdotes, quirks, professional opinions, specialist terms,
or process detail merely to make the writing appear human or expert.

## Consider the relevant reader range

Identify only materially different situations that can change the artifact.
Consider each separately rather than constructing one representative, average,
or omnibus reader.

For a specialist page:

- use every dossier materially relevant to the subject and consequential
  intersections;
- include central and contextual relevance when the page needs to serve both;
- consider differences in familiarity, purpose, language, circumstances, and
  decision only when supported and consequential; and
- do not make the most painful, stigmatised, or emotionally prominent finding
  the automatic page premise.

For a general page:

- assume a reader may have none, one, or several specialist or inclusion
  contexts relevant to the practice;
- check examples, relationship language, practical information, and
  explanations for an unstated identity, relationship, gender, family, or way
  of living as the default;
- load a specialist dossier only when it can change the copy decision; and
- mention a context only when it belongs to the subject or adds useful
  information.

Turn the range into implications for selection, terminology, explanation,
examples, emphasis, omission, or an adverse-reading check. Do not turn it into
personas, imagined testimony, one section per situation, comprehensive
coverage, automatic recognition copy, repeated reassurance, or a prescribed
emotional journey.

For narrow work, retain only the reader differences that could change the
local decision.

## Select the artifact register

Define the relationship between speaker, artifact, and reader before choosing
tone or direct address.

| Artifact | Usual job | Common model-default failure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Home or landing page | Orient and support a next judgement or action. | Emotional funnel, universal `you`, repeated reassurance. |
| Service or specialist page | Explain the subject and support fit or decision. | Generic category copy with topic nouns substituted. |
| About or practitioner page | Present selected professional perspective and relevant practice information. | Autobiography, credential inventory, self-awarded qualities. |
| Directory profile or biography | Support independent judgement in compressed space. | Landing-page persuasion or simulated intimacy. |
| Metadata or search preview | Identify the subject and relevance accurately. | Keyword stack or claim exceeding visible copy. |
| FAQ | Answer one real consequential question. | Invented objection, repetition, or SEO landfill. |
| Form, label, error, or confirmation | Predict behaviour and next action. | Warm-sounding ambiguity or hidden instruction. |
| Policy or boundary note | State arrangement, exception, consequence, and alternative when useful. | Defensive legalism or reassurance obscuring the boundary. |

Treat the table as orientation, not a template. A defined audience does not
automatically require `you`. Choose direct address, neutral explanation,
first-person professional voice, or instruction according to the real
relationship.

## Define the communication job

Write one sentence describing what the artifact must contribute here. Then
decide:

- the main proposition, question, distinction, uncertainty, or practical answer;
- what the reader should be better able to understand, judge, find, or do;
- what surrounding copy has already established;
- what belongs later, elsewhere, or nowhere;
- the natural subject and search language;
- the useful speaker-owned material available;
- the necessary limits and genuine unknowns; and
- the last useful contribution after which the copy should stop.

Do not ask one artifact to inform, reassure, differentiate, persuade, represent
every reader situation, and convert at once. Select the work it actually needs
to do.

Build search intent into the job. Use the ordinary phrase a suitable reader
would recognise; align titles, headings, openings, metadata, and links without
mechanical repetition. Do not create sections merely to hold related queries.

## Explore materially different directions

For substantial new work, consider at least two directions privately or in a
compact decision record. Change the organising principle, not just the
headline or tone.

Useful structural alternatives can include:

- direct answer versus one developed example;
- explanation versus observable process;
- one narrow proposition versus several related situations;
- sequence versus scan-friendly retrieval;
- a real distinction versus practical arrangements; or
- a boundary-led explanation versus a positive account of what is available.

For each viable direction, identify:

- the central question and main message;
- what it brings forward;
- what it leaves out or compresses;
- the likely composition;
- its fit with the reader range, page context, search language, and speaker;
  and
- the main craft or model-default risk.

Reject directions that depend on invented reader interior, decorative
specificity, generic empathy, performed expertise, promised outcomes, or
repeated corrective rhetoric. Do not draft every option or combine their
safest-looking parts.

## Make the editorial commitment

Select one direction and record, proportionately:

```text
Artifact and page position:
Reader scope and material differences:
Communication job:
Main message:
Source-backed material and voice evidence:
Editorial commitment:
What leads and receives emphasis:
What is omitted, compressed, or deferred:
Register and reader relationship:
Natural search language:
Exit or stopping point:
Likely model-default risk:
Genuine unknowns:
```

The commitment must join content and treatment. "Explain the service clearly"
is not a commitment. A useful commitment identifies what controls the piece,
how the material moves, and what will not be allowed to fill the shape.

The model-default risk must predict a generative habit and a prevention
decision. For example: "The supplied concern categories could become equal
paragraphs, so organise around the one distinction that changes the reader's
decision and compress the rest."

## Design the composition

Shape the artifact before sentences:

1. **Entry:** Put the most useful answer, subject, situation, or live tension
   where it can work without atmospheric throat-clearing.
2. **Development:** Expand, qualify, illustrate, contrast, or apply only what
   the central proposition needs.
3. **Movement:** Make each paragraph change the reader's understanding rather
   than paraphrase the preceding point.
4. **Emphasis:** Give the strongest supported idea disproportionate weight.
5. **Exit:** Stop on the last useful fact, judgement, boundary, question, or
   action. Do not manufacture a conclusion because the shape looks unfinished.

Choose a statement, explanation, example, list, comparison, question, label,
link, pause, or omission according to the material. Let importance control
length and pattern. A strong composition may be compact, cumulative,
asymmetrical, interrupted, or quiet.

For a section within a page, compose from the available preceding material into
the next known need. Do not restart the entire page proposition or invent
transitions for unavailable adjacent content.
